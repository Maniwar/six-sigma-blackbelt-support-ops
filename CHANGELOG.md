# Changelog

Release notes for the program hub. These used to live inside the page itself, which meant every reader had to scroll past the maintenance history of a document they had only just opened. They belong here.

The live version is shown under the title on the page — check it against the top entry below to see whether you are looking at the current build.

## New in v3.4 &mdash; the charts arrive, and the plumbing says what it is

- **Fixed: a benefit case that was arithmetically exact and causally impossible.** The worked project ran on `266,000 &times; 6.2 points &times; $6.80 &times; 0.85 = $95,324`, and every multiplication in it is correct. The chain is still nonsense, because the 6.2-point gap is measured on in-scope billing adjustments and the 266,000 is the whole billing queue. Adjustments are 11,592 of those 266,000 contacts and 1,646 of the queue's 37,772 reopens &mdash; 4.4% of each &mdash; so fixing *every* adjustment reopen in the year moves the queue rate from 14.2% to 13.58%. That is 0.62 points against a claimed 6.2: ten times the arithmetic maximum. What hid it is that the reopen rate is 14.2% on both populations, measured two different ways, so the figure reconciled everywhere anyone looked. The honest chain is `11,592 &times; 6.2 points = 719 &times; $38.60 = $27,753 &times; 0.85 = $23,590`, which does not clear the $50,000 floor Finance books against &mdash; and the pack now says so, in the charter, rather than quietly carrying a number that works. The unit cost moved with it: $6.80 is the cost to serve one contact and was never the price of a reopen. This is the ordinary way a benefit case dies, and the v1.0 chain is kept in the revision history instead of deleted, because recognising the shape of it is the lesson.
- **The metric dictionary now says what the numbers are.** NPS was defined as detractors subtracted from promoters, without stating that the scale is 0&ndash;10, that a promoter is a 9 or 10 and a detractor a 0 through 6, or that the 7s and 8s count in the denominator and nowhere else &mdash; which is the whole reason the arithmetic surprises people. CSAT and CES were the same shape: a name, a direction, and no scale, no cut point, no denominator. Every rate row now carries its scale, what counts as a hit, what sits in the denominator, and a worked example with real numbers.
- **Handle time has three answers, and the table gave one.** AHT was defined for voice only, so chat &mdash; where an agent holds several conversations at once &mdash; had no definition at all, and the two obvious ways to compute it disagree by exactly the concurrency factor. An agent in chat status for 20 minutes handling two chats that ran the full 20 minutes has an average handle time of 20 minutes per chat from the case's point of view and 10 from the agent's, and both are correct answers to different questions. Synchronous chat, asynchronous chat and concurrency each have their own row now, each says which point of view it takes, and the entry works through that example rather than asserting a formula. Staffing needs the agent view; a customer-facing SLA needs the case view; the identity between them (`busy &divide; chats` and `session &divide; concurrency` are the same quantity) is stated, along with the one thing that breaks it &mdash; idle time inside the concurrency denominator.
- **&ldquo;Erlang C does not model chat&rdquo; now says what to use instead.** Naming a limit and stopping there leaves the reader exactly where they started, and &ldquo;then simulate&rdquo; is not an instruction. Erlang C models one customer per server, so it cannot represent a concurrency cap &mdash; a chat waits for an agent who is *below the cap*, not for one who is free. But it still sizes the workload correctly if you feed it the agent view, because arrivals &times; agent-view AHT is agents-busy with the concurrency already divided out; feed it the case view instead and you over-staff by exactly the concurrency factor, which is the single most expensive substitution on that page. What it cannot do is tell you what the cap costs: session duration is not independent of load, so the handle time measured at a cap of 2 is not the one you get at 3, and Erlang reports neither that nor response latency &mdash; the number a chat SLA is actually written on. So size with Erlang on the agent view, treat it as a floor, and validate against a discrete-event simulation that models the cap, reading the output as service level against concurrency cap rather than as one headcount. `28-erlang-staffing.xlsx` says the same thing on the sheet, where the reader is when the question comes up.
- **Fixed: the chat handle-time entry was unreadable in the table it lives in.** Writing all the nuance down was right; pasting it flat into a 31%-wide cell was not. That one entry ran 3,262 characters against a median of 148 &mdash; twenty-two times its neighbours, 4,600 pixels tall, and a reader scrolling the dictionary hit a wall of text with no way past it. Nothing is cut: the trap itself is now two lines (*quoting a chat handle time without saying which of the three it is, then feeding the wrong one to a staffing model &mdash; one 20-minute session answers 10, 5 and 10 minutes, and all three are correct*), and the worked example, the concurrency identity, the occupancy-above-100% trap and the Erlang C replacement each sit behind a fold, in the same disclosure idiom the modules and the tool library already use. The row is 682 pixels now and is no longer the tallest in the table.
- **Fixed: a tool that would have put the withdrawn benefit case back.** `tools/md_guidance.py` fills a blank markdown template with the worked example, which means it holds a second copy of every figure the pack states &mdash; and a second copy of a number is a number that will drift. It had. It still carried `61,400 billing tickets`, `3,807 reopens avoided` and `$22,005 realized`, none of which survive anywhere else, along with `$6.80` as the unit cost and `OD-BIL-004 v2` as the project's own metric definition. Worse, it rewrote each document's opening block unconditionally, and its copy of that block said *&ldquo;a 14.2% 7-day reopen rate&rdquo;* with no population named &mdash; so one run of it would have printed, into all eleven documents, the exact sentence those documents now exist to warn against. Measured: 26 lines across 11 files, including a whole paragraph the reconciliation had added. The file is now the authority on its own prose and the block is only written where there is not one already; all 219 example values are reconciled; and the build checks that every figure the tool would write is one the pack actually states, so the next drift fails instead of waiting. Nothing invokes this tool during a build, which is exactly why it rotted unseen for a release.
- **Fixed: one workbook gave two answers to its own question.** The Black Belt calculator pack's ROI tab shipped a worked example of `$156,672` realised benefit. Two tabs earlier, &ldquo;Benefit &mdash; avoided contacts&rdquo; computes `$172,012.80` from the inputs printed beside it. Nothing said which was meant and the two were $15,340.80 apart; `$156,672` reconciles with nothing anywhere in the file. The example now carries tab 8's own output &mdash; the cell stays a yellow input, because a general calculator has to accept a benefit that came from anywhere, but a worked example may not contradict the tab next to it &mdash; and the build now ties the two together so they cannot drift apart again.
- **Fixed: a calculator that quietly taught the mistake this programme exists to catch.** The same tab invites you to reduce a &ldquo;reopen rate, escalation rate, repeat-contact rate &mdash; whatever you are reducing&rdquo;, and then priced whatever you removed at *cost to serve an average contact*. A reopen is not an average contact: it carries the investigation and the redo on top of the handling, and in this pack's own worked project the two rates are $6.80 and $38.60 &mdash; 5.7&times; apart, with the cheap one being precisely the substitution its charter records as fatal. The cost-basis note now makes the distinction, tells you to ask Finance which rate applies to the thing you are actually removing, and says that a rate from one population against a volume from a wider one is how a benefit case comes out ten times its arithmetic maximum.
- **Fixed: four control charts that never caught anything, and one that caught the wrong thing.** A control chart is a signal detector, and four of the seven shipped worked data so quiet that the Signal column was blank down all 24 rows &mdash; so a reader learned the shape of the chart and nothing about reading one, and the rule formula had never once been evaluated against a true case. It could have been broken since the day it was written. Each now does what a real chart does: the baseline window stays in control, because limits computed from an unstable process are not limits, and the shift lands in the monitoring window after it. Daily AHT steps past the I-MR limit while the moving range stays put, which is the whole reason there are two charts and not one; service level falls through the p&prime; lower limit; the audit defect rate triples through the u&prime; upper one; and the rare-event chart trips its **upper** limit, because that is the improvement signal and a reader who only ever sees a chart catch bad news learns it is an alarm rather than a detector.
- **Fixed: an Xbar-R sheet computing its limits from a process it showed was out of control.** It signalled three times *inside* its own 20-point baseline window &mdash; the one error the method most needs a reader to avoid, shipped as the worked example. Its subgroups were not five observations either: they came from `sin(i x 1.7 + j x 2.1)`, and since j reaches 6.3 against a period of 6.283, observation 4 repeated observation 1 and observation 5 repeated observation 2 in nearly every row &mdash; `[437, 437, 404, 437, 437]`. The R chart exists to measure within-subgroup spread and was being handed three distinct numbers padded out to five. Regenerated whole. Both faults are now checked on every build, and both mutants that reintroduce them are killed.
- **Every cross-reference is resolved on every build.** The eleven documents cite each other by line number, and a line number is not a stable address: one editing pass added seventeen lines to the charter and silently broke references in four sibling files. 163 of them are now checked, on the rule that if the citing sentence carries a figure, the line it points at has to carry that figure too &mdash; the failure observed every time was a citation offered as the provenance of 14.2% landing on prose with no number on it. It found four real breaks on its first run, two of which a careful reader had raised independently.
- **Two gates that were not gates.** The citation resolver above was written, wired into the build, and never called &mdash; `main()` invokes each test by name and this one was never added to the list, so for a release the harness printed PASSED with the same confidence it prints everything else. Separately, a workbook mutant had been failing with a `NameError` against twenty of the twenty-one workbooks; a mutant that raises was printed as a note and not counted, so the suite reported every mutant killed while a fifth of them never ran. Both are the same defect &mdash; silent absence reading as success &mdash; and both now fail the build. Coverage went from 221 mutants to 280.
- **Fixed: a check that had been red so long it was furniture.** 37 cells across two workbooks evaluated to `#N/A` on every run. They are supposed to: `NA()` is the only way to leave a genuine gap in a plotted series, and a Pareto's cumulative line would otherwise dive to zero across its unused rows. The exemption was a hand-written map of column spans, and these two workbooks were never added to it. A permanently red gate costs exactly what a permanently green one does, so the rule is now a property of the cell rather than a list somebody must remember to update: a deliberate gap *calls* `NA()`, an accidental one falls out of a lookup that missed. The mutant that guards it leaves the cell reporting the very same `#N/A` and changes nothing but the reason.
- **Fixed: the Word export had no charts in it.** The business case draws four, and the Word copy carried their titles, their labels and their numbers with a blank column down the middle where every bar should have been. A bar is an empty div with a background colour, the docx writer only ever collected text, and an empty element has none. Bars are redrawn as shaded table cells now. Nothing had ever run that writer: the harness stops at the docx marker because the writer needs a DOM and node has not got one, so the export went untested from the day it was written. It is driven in a real browser now and both outputs are measured.
- **Fixed: negative bars a fifth of their true length, on screen and in every email.** Found while fixing the above, and the worse of the two. A bar that crosses zero is drawn inside one half of a split cell, and its width was written out as a percentage of the whole axis rather than of that half. On the benefit bridge that drew the realisation haircut &mdash; a fifth of the gross &mdash; at a thirtieth of the bar beside it, in the one chart a Finance partner reads hardest. Measured in the browser it was 20 pixels where it should have been 96.
- **The rows that feed a chart now say what they are.** The SLA capability sheet ends in 41 rows of formulas whose third column is blank on 40 of them. It is blank on purpose &mdash; the marker series carries a value only on the row the limit falls on, and that single point is what draws the red line &mdash; but nothing said so, and a reader who cannot tell plumbing from content has to assume the blanks are cells they failed to fill in. Same for the ROI breakeven column of zeros, the sample-size marker, and the Gage R&amp;R row that read &ldquo;30% line | 30% | 30% | 30% | 30%&rdquo;.
- **Two new workbooks, for the only two tools that had none.** `31-multi-vari.xlsx` partitions variation into families from a balanced nested sample, showing its working: every sum of squares is built from a gap visible in the column above it, and the four add back to the total exactly. It also lands a variance component on zero and says plainly that a clamped component means the sample cannot separate teams from the agents inside them, not that the teams are alike. `32-system-hop.xlsx` counts the swivel chair &mdash; one row per move between applications, timed at the desk, turned into seconds per contact, a share of handle time, agent hours a year and a named pair of systems with an owner, with re-keyed fields counted separately because each one is both the waste and a defect opportunity.
- **Fixed: two charts that exaggerated what they showed.** Excel floors a bar chart just under its smallest value, so 344 seconds against 538 drew as though one agent were four times another. Seconds start at nothing whatever the data does, which makes the bound a fact about the measurement rather than a framing of the example &mdash; the only kind this pack fixes. The preview renderer then kept the tick step it had chosen for the original range, so a forced floor put twenty-eight gridlines between 0 and 140.
- **Fixed: the page had been counting itself wrong.** The schema block, the meta description and the download button all said 28 templates and 17 Excel workbooks against a registry holding 30 and 19 &mdash; stale since the regression and Gage R&amp;R workbooks landed, with nothing comparing the two. It says 32 and 21 now, and a check compares them.
- **Fixed: fifteen definitions that had been written, shipped and were unreachable.** The glossary is one JSON block plus eight `Object.assign` blocks, and a term written into two of them does not raise anything &mdash; the later one silently wins. Fifteen terms were defined twice, and it was not consistently the better definition that survived. The Tukey entry that won gave a Minitab menu path; the one it had been quietly overwriting explained that comparing five queues means ten pairwise tests and produces a false positive roughly 40% of the time, and told you to act on the intervals that exclude zero rather than on the p-value. Every pair is merged now, field by field, keeping the stronger text from each and nothing newly invented &mdash; and the build fails if a term is ever defined twice again. It also removed a wrong link: Benjamini-Hochberg was listed as another name for Bonferroni, so the page pointed at the correction it is an alternative to.
- **Fixed: the page had been miscounting its own glossary too.** 277 plain-English definitions claimed against 271 that exist &mdash; the gap was exactly the duplicates being collapsed. The claim is derived from the glossary and checked on every build.
- **A check that called real data boilerplate.** The rule against pasting one sentence onto every row of a block fired on the system-hop log, where the same four-hop loop recurs on every contact because every contact made it &mdash; there the repetition is the finding. The first attempt at an exemption was a share threshold, and the mutation suite killed it inside a minute by letting a genuinely pasted note through. What separates the two is whether the rest of the row repeats with it.

## New in v3.3 — the page stops claiming things nobody could check

Every item below is the same shape: something the program asserted &mdash; a colour&rsquo;s meaning, a certification section, a preview matching its workbook, a build diff meaning a change &mdash; with nothing able to contradict it. Three of them were introduced by the fixes in v3.2.

- **Fixed: the worked example was painted the colour that means &ldquo;type here&rdquo;.** Every legend in the pack promises yellow is yours to fill in and green is the example; on several sheets the example was yellow, so the rows you should not have to invent looked exactly like the rows you were being asked to complete. The whole example is green now, and the example ends where the blank rows begin &mdash; the Pareto ships one billing dataset across five rows, and colouring the first green and the next four yellow said the last four were yours to make up. Getting there took three attempts and the two wrong ones are opposite errors worth recording, because a machine caught both. The first painted every yellow cell in the block whatever lay beneath it, which ran past the end of the logistic tab&rsquo;s data and turned its threshold and AUC settings into what looks like reference data. The second over-corrected to the first row alone, which split every seeded example down the middle.
- **The email and standalone exports carry the charts.** The export walked the preview&rsquo;s tables and never its charts, so a workbook whose whole point is a control chart exported as a wall of numbers. The SVG carries its own styling, so it survives being pasted anywhere; a mail client that strips inline SVG lands back where the export already was rather than breaking, and the standalone page keeps them. The closing note was stale too &mdash; it explained yellow and blue and had never learned that green now means the worked example you replace.
- **Chart labels are checked for contrast.** The visual layer asserted that text is inside the frame and not printed over another label, and never that it is readable. Text on the white plot area was never the risk &mdash; the label grey is 5.8:1 and the title 17:1. The risk is a value label sitting ON a bar, where the background is that series&rsquo; own colour: dark ink on a mid-green bar is 4.4:1, under the bar, while every structural check still passes because the text exists, sits inside the frame and overlaps nothing. Checked against WCAG AA now, with the threshold derived from each label&rsquo;s declared font rather than assumed &mdash; the first version handed the title and the value labels the 3:1 large-text allowance, which at 14px and 10.5px neither is anywhere near entitled to.
- **Fixed: a scatter whose key showed a line.** The stakeholder map plots each name as a point in an influence-against-support quadrant, and its legend drew a line swatch &mdash; which says the dots are joined. They are not, and the entire reading of that chart is which quadrant a name lands in, not any path through them.
- **Fixed: occupancy of 6000%.** The Erlang staffing curve reports a line per agent count, and for every count below the offered load it showed a service level of 0.0% beside an occupancy of 6000%, 3000%, 2000% &mdash; arithmetically load divided by agents, and meaningless, because below the offered load the queue never clears and there is no steady state to be occupied in. Service level and average speed of answer both already went blank there; occupancy alone did not. Sixty rows of it read as a broken sheet.
- **Fixed: a paragraph in a column too narrow to read it.** Notes are measured by the lines they wrap to now, not their character count, and widened across the row when there is empty space to widen into. The worst was the control plan: its six hierarchy definitions, 130&ndash;170 characters each in a column 26 wide, each wrapping six or seven lines deep so the block read as shredded text. A flat character threshold had missed all six &mdash; the same sentence is comfortable in a wide column and unreadable in a narrow one.
- **Fixed: chart labels cut mid-word, and a colour that only existed in the download.** &ldquo;Delighters &mdash; differentiatio&rdquo; reads as a broken renderer rather than a label too long for its axis; truncation now stops at a word boundary and says it happened. And the countermeasure hierarchy&rsquo;s green-amber-red durability signal, which lives in the font, was dropped by the preview renderer &mdash; so it was real in Excel and invisible on the page.
- **Fixed: a formula painted as something to type over.** Each colour has one job, and two of them are instructions &mdash; yellow says type here, green says this is the example, replace it. Both are wrong on a cell the workbook computes, and following either destroys the calculation. 55 cells across six workbooks carried one, including the Share and Rank on the Pareto&rsquo;s own worked-example row. Every colour check until now asked what a fill means for guidance, never whether it contradicts what the cell contains.
- **Fixed: blank rows counted as worked example.** Entry grids number their rows in advance and carry a formula on every row whether or not it has data, so a row holding nothing but the number 17 and a column of `IF(...,"")` looked full. That made the hypothesis log a 23-row worked example on the strength of the numbers 6 to 23, and the value stream map a 19-row one over nine real steps. A row is part of the example only if somebody typed something into it. The per-sheet legends on seven workbooks said &ldquo;Row 1 example &mdash; delete it&rdquo;, which was true of none of them.
- **A sheet that is all worked example has to say so.** The guidance check counted input cells per workbook, so a sheet could lose every one it had and the file still passed on the strength of a different tab. But &ldquo;every sheet needs a yellow cell&rdquo; turned out to be the wrong rule too: some sheets are entirely a worked example on purpose &mdash; 24 periods of control-chart data, 90 Gage R&amp;R readings, 40 handle times whose whole job is to show you a skewed distribution. What has to hold is that the sheet and its instructions agree, so an all-green sheet must be one the workbook tells you to overwrite. Three sheets said to overwrite a yellow column that no longer existed on them, and the legend said &ldquo;Green row&rdquo;, singular, and &ldquo;delete it when you start&rdquo; &mdash; which on a control chart leaves an empty chart. All of them now say the green cells are the example and the thing you replace.
- **Fixed: a row with a name and a number and nothing else.** &ldquo;DPO | 0.0507&rdquo;, and no indication of what it is or what to do about it. On the page every acronym is clickable; in a downloaded workbook nothing is. 77 rows across four workbooks &mdash; most of the Black Belt calculator pack, and the six levels of the hierarchy of controls, which the control plan charts but never defined. 73 now carry a note; the other four are verdicts and problem statements whose value is already a full sentence.
- **Fixed: three tools pointed at a template that could not do them.** &ldquo;Linear and multiple regression&rdquo;, &ldquo;Logistic regression&rdquo; and &ldquo;Multicollinearity check&rdquo; all linked to a narrative evidence pack with nowhere to write a coefficient. The build now checks that a tool&rsquo;s template mentions that tool.
- **Fixed: the certification mapping cited sections that do not exist.** Every module declares which parts of the ASQ and IASSC bodies of knowledge it covers, and nothing had ever checked those claims against the published outlines. Six of the cited ASQ sections exist in neither the 2015 nor the 2022 body of knowledge &mdash; I.C, I.D, I.E, III.E, VII.D, VIII.E &mdash; along with VI.C.5 and VII.B.5. Several more existed but meant something other than the label written beside them: &ldquo;IV.B (VOC)&rdquo; where IV.B is the business case and voice of the customer is IV.A; &ldquo;V.E (MSA)&rdquo; where V.E is probability and measurement system analysis is V.C; &ldquo;VI.D (correlation, linear/multiple regression)&rdquo; where VI.D is gap, root cause and waste analysis. The drift was systematic rather than scattered: the Define tollgate pointed at Measure sections, the DOE module at Lean methods, hypothesis testing at risk analysis. All 26 modules are re-mapped against the published outlines &mdash; and every code now carries its section title, so a reader meeting &ldquo;ASQ V.F&rdquo; is told it means process capability. The three tollgate clinics, which carried no mapping at all, have one now; that omission is what hid the drift, because any tool reading the page top to bottom paired each clinic's neighbour with the wrong module.
- **The three sections the programme did not teach, now taught.** Mapping the curriculum honestly turned up three ASQ sections with no module behind them: benchmarking, team training, and design for X. Rather than narrow the claim, they are written. M05 gains the four types of benchmarking and, more usefully, how you turn a benchmark into a defensible target &mdash; you do not copy the number, you size the gap with it and set the target from your own baseline and entitlement. M06 gains training the three audiences every project has (the team at kickoff, the process owner at handover, the front line each time a control changes), where mode of learning is a staffing decision because every hour off the phone is coverage lost. M26 gains the support translation of design for X: design for cost becomes cost-to-serve, manufacturability becomes self-service and containment, test becomes whether an agent can tell what went wrong without escalating. Coverage is 33 of 33 ASQ sections and 21 of 21 IASSC sections, and the page says so only because the build derives it from the modules.
- **A body of knowledge coverage table.** Each module stated its own mapping, but you could only ever see them one expanded module at a time, so &ldquo;full ASQ/IASSC body of knowledge&rdquo; was a claim with no way to audit it. There is now one table under Reference showing every section of both outlines against the module that teaches it, with each module a link into the module itself. It is generated from the same data the module lines are, so a section nothing covered would appear in it saying so &mdash; which is how the three gaps above were found.
- **The preview shows what the workbook actually says.** Nineteen previews were hand-written markup that the sync could only edit in place &mdash; it rewrites the cells already present, so it could correct a value but never add a row. Anything added to those workbooks afterwards was in the file you downloaded and nowhere on the page: 31 pieces of prose, including all six definitions of the hierarchy of controls and nineteen of the notes just written into the calculator pack. Every preview is now generated from its workbook, the way the newer templates always were, so the two agree by construction rather than by whoever remembered to update both &mdash; and the build fails if they ever drift.
- **The build is byte-reproducible.** An .xlsx records the time in three independent places, and every one of them was the clock, so a rebuild that changed nothing still produced twenty modified binary files and a page diff to match. `git diff` could not answer &ldquo;did that build do anything?&rdquo; &mdash; and it was hiding two things that had been true for as long as this repo has existed: a chart was re-anchored on every run, and a note&rsquo;s font rewritten on every run, growing one workbook&rsquo;s style table by an orphaned entry per build, forever. All three timestamps are pinned and both churning writes now only fire on a real change.
- **The harness grew from 147 mutants to 199, and two of them exist because a check shipped that could not fail.** The rule that says no two chart labels may print through each other passed everything on its first run, and the mutant written to break it survived: the rule was reading its own coordinates against the wrong elements. Chasing it, the second mutant turned out to be invalid too &mdash; a label slid into empty space collides with nothing. Both the bug and the bad mutant that hid it are written up in the code, because &ldquo;the mutant was wrong&rdquo; is the more dangerous conclusion: it is the one that talks you out of a real finding.

## New in v3.2 — you can see the charts, and the templates explain themselves

- **Every chart is drawn in the preview.** All 38 native Excel charts are redrawn as inline SVG under the sheet they belong to, from the same recalculated cells the table above shows. They were downloadable but not viewable before, so every chart fix this project has made was invisible to anyone who previewed a template rather than opening it in Excel — and nothing compared the two, so nothing ever said so. `tools/verify.py` now counts a workbook's charts against its preview's `<svg>` tags on every build.
- **The markdown templates are audited now.** Every layer of the quality harness globbed `*.xlsx`, so no check had ever opened one of the eleven documents. The new layer found 97 defects on its first run: **22 tables declared and then shipped completely empty** across ten of the eleven documents, and four documents asking for a kappa, a p-value, a power calculation or a variance component without saying anywhere how to obtain one. All 97 are fixed — every table now carries a worked example from the one billing-adjustment project that runs through the pack.
- **A new workbook: continuous Gage R&amp;R.** `29-msa-gage-rr.xlsx` is the crossed ANOVA the continuous MSA record never had. Ninety readings in; variance components, % contribution, % study variation and ndc out, with every sum of squares written as a formula you can point at. It recalculates to 28.4% study variation and ndc 4 — exactly the figures the document reports, so the two cannot disagree.
- **Fixed: calculated cells showed no number in the preview.** They rendered as empty blue boxes with a small &ldquo;fx&rdquo;, because a formula's displayed value came from a list maintained by hand and anything added later was simply missing from it. The values come from a real recalculation now.
- **Fixed: regenerated previews were being dropped.** The builder rebuilds these previews from the workbook on every run — and nothing carried the result into the page. Eight previews were carrying markup older than the workbooks they describe.
- **Fixed: label thinning that hid labels which fit.** It was sized off the rows a chart *reserves* rather than the rows it *fills*, so the value stream map showed one step in three with room for all nine, and its exemption for ranked bar charts tested a property that never matched, so every vertical column chart was thinned anyway. Thinning is now sized on label width: twenty-five test numbers fit where twenty-four &ldquo;Day 12&rdquo;s do not.
- **Fixed: the SLA chart had no red line.** Its caption read &ldquo;area past the red line is a breach&rdquo;, and the line was a single point with no marker — which Excel and LibreOffice both draw as nothing at all.
- **Fixed: bar charts lost the meaning their colours carried.** The Kano categories, the control hierarchy and the components of variation all rendered as one flat blue in the preview.
- **A new workbook: regression.** &ldquo;Linear and multiple regression&rdquo; and &ldquo;Logistic regression&rdquo; both linked to the root-cause evidence pack &mdash; a narrative document with nowhere to write a coefficient, an R&sup2;, a VIF or a residual. `30-regression.xlsx` is what those tools needed: simple linear with the fitted line over the scatter; multiple regression with per-coefficient t and p, a VIF for each predictor, and residuals against fitted, which is the plot that decides whether the model may be used at all; and a logistic tab that is honest about its limits &mdash; plain formulas cannot do maximum likelihood, so it takes the coefficients you paste from Minitab or R and does the interpretation around them.
- **Every acronym in a workbook is explained now.** On the page each one is clickable; in a downloaded file nothing is, so a reader met &ldquo;MR of z&rdquo;, &ldquo;A2 for this n&rdquo; and &ldquo;Obs 1&rdquo; with no way to guess. 37 column headers used a term this program defines in its own glossary and said nothing about it anywhere on the sheet. Each affected table now carries a plain-English key above it.

## New in v3.1 — the charts stopped contradicting their own sheets

- **Every control chart now marks its own signals.** Ten charts, and not one of them showed which point was out of control — the most standard element of a control chart, absent from all of them. Points outside the limits are flagged red; eight in a row on one side is flagged amber. Deliberately only those two rules: the full Nelson set across ten charts drives the false-alarm rate to roughly one signal per chart per fortnight on a perfectly stable process, which is exactly the tampering the workbook warns against. Two of the ten needed different maths — gaps between rare events are exponential and opportunities-between-events are geometric, so P(above the mean) is about 0.368 rather than 0.5, and a naive run rule there advertises odds it does not deliver. ## In v2.4 — four more ways a support project makes money

- **Deflect to self-service or a cheaper channel.** The contact still happens, it just does not need a person. The benefit is the cost *difference*, never the whole contact cost — a distinction the old “eliminate a contact driver” model could not express.
- **Reduce agent attrition.** Recruiting, onboarding, training and the ramp you pay for twice. At 30–45% annual attrition this is often the largest number in a support P&L, and it is the only model here priced per person rather than per contact.
- **Cut the cost of poor quality.** Credits, refunds, goodwill and SLA penalties. Priced per incident, so a small defect-rate move can dwarf a big handle-time project — and the money is already in Finance's ledger, so you are not asking them to believe a model.
- **Protect revenue at risk.** The only revenue model rather than cost. Priced per customer, not per contact, because one customer raises several tickets — and the wizard now asks for customers so you cannot accidentally multiply the benefit by your contacts-per-customer ratio.
