Why support operations breaks textbook Six Sigma

Most Black Belt training is written for a factory. Copy it into a contact center unchanged and it fails in five predictable ways. The curriculum below is designed around these five, and instructors should name them explicitly on day one.

1. The measurement system is human

In a plant, a caliper measures a shaft. In support, a QA analyst scores a call, an agent picks a disposition code, and a customer answers a survey. All three are subjective instruments with real repeatability and reproducibility error. Attribute Agreement Analysis is the single highest-value tool in this program — and it is the one most programs skip. Expect first-pass kappa between QA analysts of 0.35–0.60 where the org believed it was near 1.0.

2. Almost no support data is normal

Handle time, hold time, resolution time and queue time are right-skewed, usually approximately lognormal, frequently multi-modal because several distinct contact reasons are mixed together. Running a t-test on raw AHT is the most common analytical error in support Six Sigma. Non-parametric tests, transformation, and — most importantly — stratification before testing are core competencies, not appendices.

3. Demand is exogenous and highly variable

A factory sets its own takt. A support team receives arrivals it does not control, with intraday, day-of-week and seasonal patterns plus product-release shocks. Queueing behaviour (Erlang C, Little's Law) is non-linear near saturation: at 90% occupancy a 5% demand increase can double wait time. Black Belts must be able to separate process variation from arrival variation before they attribute a change to their improvement.

4. The "defect" is contested

There is no spec drawing. Is a transfer a defect? A reopen? A 4-out-of-5 CSAT? A contact that should never have happened? Programs stall for months arguing this. The program forces a decision in Define via an explicit operational definition, and requires the Champion to sign it.

5. Improvements decay

A poka-yoke welded into a fixture stays. A new agent behaviour decays within 6–10 weeks under attrition, new-hire dilution and competing priorities. Control plans in support must lean on system-enforced controls (routing rules, required fields, guided workflows, forced-choice macros) rather than training and reminders. Grade control plans by how system-enforced they are.

Bonus: the AI-era complication

Deflection by bots and AI assist changes the mix of what reaches humans: easy contacts leave the queue first, so human AHT and CSAT can worsen while total cost improves. Any before/after comparison must control for contact-mix shift — usually via stratified analysis or a mix-adjusted index. Teach this as a standard confounder, in Analyze.

Belt architecture

Black Belts do not work in isolation. The program only produces sustained results when the supporting belt population exists to feed projects, staff teams and hold gains. Recommended ratios assume a support organization of 500–2,000 FTE; scale linearly.

Sizing rule of thumb

A Black Belt working at 100% dedication should deliver 3–5 completed projects per year at a $150k–$250k average validated annual benefit. A Black Belt at 25% dedication delivers roughly one project a year and usually abandons it. Partial dedication is the most reliable predictor of program failure — treat <50% as a red flag at charter.

Wave 1  Define, deployment and team leadership · 5 days · Modules 1–7

Wave 1 exists to stop the two failure modes that kill more support Six Sigma projects than any statistical error: a badly-scoped problem, and a Black Belt with no organizational leverage.

M01Six Sigma, Lean and the support operating system0.5 day
BOK mapping

ASQ I.A–I.C (organization-wide considerations, leadership, deployment) · IASSC 1.1, 1.4

Learning objectives
  • Explain the history and logic of Six Sigma, Lean, TQM and the Theory of Constraints, and where each is the right tool
  • Articulate Y = f(x₁ … xₙ) and why support organizations chronically manage Y (CSAT, AHT, SLA) instead of the x's
  • Position DMAIC against agile, kaizen events, A3, and "just fix it" — and choose correctly
  • Describe how a support improvement program interacts with WFM, QA, Training, Product and Engineering
Support application

Case study: an org that spent three years running weekly AHT reviews with team leaders. AHT was the Y. Nobody could name the x's. Sigma-level thinking reframes the same data as a variance-reduction problem — the top decile of handle times, not the mean, is where the money is.

Exercise

Each participant writes the Y = f(x) equation for their own project, listing at least eight candidate x's and classifying each as controllable, noise, or SOP-fixed. Peer review in pairs.

Common pitfall

Participants list other departments as x's ("Product ships bugs"). Push them to decompose into something measurable and at least partially within reach.

M02Voice of the customer, business and employee0.75 day
BOK mapping

ASQ IV.B (VOC) · IASSC 1.1, 1.2

Learning objectives
  • Design and execute VOC collection: survey design, interviews, focus groups, contact-driver mining, verbatim analysis
  • Distinguish stated needs, unstated needs and delighters (Kano model) and apply Kano to support experiences
  • Build a CTQ tree from a vague customer statement to a measurable specification
  • Recognize survey bias: non-response bias, solicitation timing, scale anchoring, agent-solicited survey gaming
  • Compare CSAT, NPS and CES and explain what each is and is not sensitive to
Support application

Support has an unusual VOC asset almost no other function has: millions of recorded verbatims — chat transcripts, call recordings, survey comments, ticket bodies. Teach topic modelling / clustering of verbatims as a first-class VOC method alongside surveys.

Critical bias to teach: CSAT respondents are typically 5–20% of contacts and are strongly bimodal (delighted and furious). Median satisfaction of the silent 80–95% is unknown. Never treat CSAT as a population parameter without a non-response analysis.

CTQ tree example
Need: "I don't want to explain my problem twice" Driver: Context is preserved across transfers and channels CTQ: % of transferred contacts where receiving agent restates the issue correctly without asking the customer to repeat Spec: ≥ 95% (measured by QA sampling, n=200/month, Tier-1→Tier-2 transfers)
Exercise

Teams take 40 real survey verbatims (supplied), affinity-diagram them, build a Kano classification, and derive three CTQs with operational definitions and measurement methods.

Common pitfall

Stopping the CTQ tree at the driver level ("better communication"). A CTQ must name the metric, the population, the sampling method and the target. If two people could measure it differently, it is not finished.

The AHT-savings trap

"We cut AHT by 12 seconds across 4M contacts, therefore we saved 13,300 hours, therefore we saved $X." This is only true if the capacity is actually harvested. If schedules are unchanged, the savings become occupancy reduction — real for agents, invisible to the P&L. Require every AHT project to state the harvest mechanism (headcount reduction, hiring avoidance, outsourcer volume renegotiation, or absorbed growth) in the charter, signed by Finance and WFM.

Exercise

Participants rebuild their own charter's benefit model in the supplied workbook, then defend it to a mock Finance partner played by the instructor. Most charters lose 40–60% of their claimed benefit in this exercise. That is the point.

Not-DMAIC screen
  • Cause is already known and solution is obvious → just do it
  • Process does not exist yet → DFSS / DMADV (Module 26)
  • Needs a technology purchase decision → business case, not DMAIC
  • Scope spans >3 departments with no executive sponsor → too big; break it up
  • Fewer than ~30 events per month in the metric → too little data to detect change; consider qualitative methods
M04Process mapping: SIPOC, swimlanes and value stream maps0.75 day
BOK mapping

ASQ II.A, IV.C, V.A · IASSC 2.1

Learning objectives
  • Build SIPOC at the right altitude to set scope boundaries
  • Construct swimlane maps that expose handoffs, decision points and rework loops
  • Build a value stream map with process time, lead time, %C&A (complete and accurate) and calculate Process Cycle Efficiency
  • Distinguish value-add, business-value-add and non-value-add activity from the customer's perspective

Support application

The support value stream is measured in elapsed time, but only a fraction is touch time. Typical findings when a team first maps a Tier-1→Tier-2 ticket:

Lead time (created → resolved) 38.5 hours Touch time (sum of agent handling) 0.72 hours Process Cycle Efficiency = 0.72 / 38.5 = 1.9% Waiting states consuming the other 98.1%: awaiting customer reply 19.1 h in Tier-2 queue, unassigned 9.8 h awaiting engineering triage 6.4 h awaiting internal approval 2.5 h

This single calculation reframes almost every support improvement conversation: the opportunity is in the queue, not in the agent.

M06Team leadership, stakeholders and change management1 day
BOK mapping

ASQ III.A–III.E (team formation, facilitation, dynamics, training, tools) · I.E change management

Learning objectives
  • Form and launch a project team: RACI, ground rules, meeting cadence, decision rights
  • Facilitate: nominal group technique, multivoting, affinity diagrams, brainwriting, structured debate
  • Diagnose team dysfunction against Tuckman stages and intervene
  • Run stakeholder analysis (influence/interest grid) and build a targeted communication plan
  • Apply a change model (ADKAR or Kotter) to a support-floor rollout

Support application

The highest-risk stakeholder in a support Six Sigma project is almost never the customer or the executive — it is the front-line supervisor, whose bonus is tied to metrics the project will temporarily worsen. A project that reduces contact volume will raise per-contact AHT (easy contacts leave first) and can dent occupancy. If nobody has renegotiated the supervisor's targets in advance, the improvement will be quietly sabotaged.

Mandatory charter clause

Every charter must include a metric-impact disclosure: which existing team or individual scorecards this project will move, in which direction, and who has agreed to adjust targets. Unsigned, the project does not start.

Exercise

Role-play: participant presents their project to a supervisor (played by a peer) who has been briefed to object on the grounds of headcount fear and AHT impact. Debriefed against a resistance taxonomy.

M08Data types, sampling and data collection plans0.75 day
BOK mapping

ASQ V.A, V.B · IASSC 3.2

Learning objectives
  • Classify data: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio; continuous vs. discrete; and choose analysis accordingly
  • Design sampling schemes: simple random, stratified, systematic, cluster; and know when each is appropriate
  • Compute sample size for means, proportions and attribute agreement studies
  • Build a data collection plan: what, how, who, when, how many, stratification factors
  • Identify and mitigate sampling bias in operational data
Support application — the stratification factors that matter

Nearly every support analysis is confounded unless stratified by some combination of: contact reason / intent, channel, customer segment or tier, product or SKU, agent tenure band, site or vendor, shift and day-part, language, new vs. returning issue, and whether the contact was bot-deflected first. Teach participants to build the stratification list before pulling data, not after the first surprising result.

Operational-data traps specific to support
  • Survivorship in CSAT. Only responders are in the data, and abandoned contacts never get surveyed at all.
  • Timestamp fiction. "Resolved" timestamps are often set in bulk by an automation or a nightly auto-close job. Check for suspicious clustering at :00 seconds and at scheduled job times.
  • Disposition drift. Contact-reason codes are chosen by agents under time pressure. The top code is frequently "Other" or whatever is first alphabetically in the dropdown.
  • Pause abuse. Any SLA clock that can be paused will be paused. Compare paused vs. unpaused duration distributions.
  • Merged/duplicate tickets. Merges silently delete units from the denominator and inflate FCR.
  • Backfill. Historical records get edited. Snapshot your baseline extract and store it immutably.
Exercise

Participants pull 30 days of their own project data and run a supplied data-audit script that checks for each of the six traps above. Every cohort finds at least two.

M09Descriptive statistics and graphical analysis0.75 day
BOK mapping

ASQ V.C, V.D · IASSC 2.2

Learning objectives
  • Compute and interpret measures of central tendency, dispersion, skewness and kurtosis
  • Read histograms, boxplots, run charts, scatterplots, Pareto charts, dot plots and violin plots
  • Test for normality (Anderson–Darling, Shapiro–Wilk, probability plots) and interpret the p-value correctly at large n
  • Identify and handle outliers without simply deleting them
  • Build multi-vari and interval plots
Support application

The mean is almost always the wrong statistic for support duration data. A right-skewed AHT distribution with a mean of 7:12 might have a median of 5:40 and a 95th percentile of 22:30. The customers who suffer are in the tail; the mean hides them, and the mean is what the dashboard shows.

Teach the standard reporting discipline: for any duration metric, report median, 90th percentile and the shape of the distribution — never the mean alone.

Normality testing at large n

With n = 50,000 contacts, Anderson–Darling will return p < 0.005 for a distribution that is visually near-normal and practically fine. The test detects trivial departures. Teach participants to judge normality from the probability plot and histogram, and to use the formal test as a supporting input at moderate n — not as an automatic gate. Conversely, do not assume normality just because n is large: the CLT applies to the sampling distribution of the mean, not to the raw data, and it does nothing for capability analysis or percentile estimates.

Exercise

Given a mixed AHT dataset, participants discover the bimodality, stratify by contact reason to resolve it, and demonstrate that the "AHT problem" is entirely a mix problem in two of the six contact types.

M10Measurement System Analysis I — attribute agreement1 day
BOK mapping

ASQ V.E (MSA) · IASSC 2.3

The highest-value day in the program

Most support organizations have never validated their QA scoring, disposition coding or intent tagging. This module routinely produces the single most useful finding of an entire Black Belt project, before any improvement work begins.

Learning objectives
  • Distinguish accuracy, precision, bias, linearity, stability, repeatability and reproducibility
  • Design an attribute agreement study: appraisers, sample selection, replication, blinding, randomization
  • Compute and interpret within-appraiser agreement, between-appraiser agreement, agreement with a known standard, and Cohen's / Fleiss' kappa
  • Apply kappa interpretation thresholds and know their limitations (prevalence and bias paradoxes)
  • Design corrective action: rubric revision, calibration cadence, forced-choice redesign, examples library
Study design for QA scoring
Sample: 50 contacts, deliberately stratified — 20 clear pass, 20 clear fail, 10 genuinely ambiguous (do NOT use a random sample; you need failure cases to have any power to detect disagreement) Appraisers: all QA analysts scoring that queue, minimum 3 Replicates: 2 per appraiser, separated by ≥ 5 days, randomized order, blinded to prior score Standard: consensus score agreed by a senior panel, established before the study and not shown to appraisers Output: within-appraiser %, between-appraiser %, vs-standard %, Fleiss' kappa overall and per-rubric-item
KappaInterpretationAction in a support context
> 0.90ExcellentAcceptable; monitor with periodic calibration
0.75 – 0.90GoodUsable; target the specific rubric items with lowest agreement
0.40 – 0.75MarginalDo not use for individual performance management. Fix the rubric before using the data.
< 0.40PoorMeasurement system is not fit for purpose. Halt any use of this data in decisions.
Two traps

The kappa prevalence paradox: if 95% of sampled contacts pass, two appraisers who both say "pass" to everything will show 95% raw agreement and near-zero kappa. This is why the study sample must be stratified to include failures — and why you report raw agreement and kappa together.

Rubric items are not equally reliable. Report kappa per rubric line. "Verified customer identity" typically scores >0.9; "demonstrated empathy" typically scores 0.2–0.4. The fix is not more calibration meetings — it is rewriting or removing the unmeasurable item.

Exercise

Live study run during the wave: instructor supplies 30 anonymized chat transcripts; participants score them against the org's real QA rubric; results are computed in class. The cohort's own kappa is the teaching material.

M11Measurement System Analysis II — continuous Gage R&R and data lineage0.5 day
BOK mapping

ASQ V.E · IASSC 2.3

Learning objectives
  • Run and interpret a crossed Gage R&R (ANOVA method): %contribution, %study variation, number of distinct categories
  • Apply the <10% / 10–30% / >30% study-variation decision rule
  • Assess bias, linearity and stability of an instrument
  • Audit a data pipeline as a measurement system: source system → ETL → warehouse → dashboard
Support application

Continuous Gage R&R has fewer natural applications in support than attribute studies, but two matter a great deal:

  • Time-and-motion observation. If observers are stopwatching agent tasks for a value stream map, run a Gage R&R across observers before trusting the data. Reproducibility between observers is usually worse than expected on task-boundary judgements.
  • Timestamp-derived metrics. Treat the reporting pipeline itself as the gage. Support specs are typically one-sided:

The large-n problem — the defining statistical hazard of support analytics

A contact center generating 3 million contacts a year can detect a 2-second AHT difference at p < 0.001. It is real. It is also worth nothing. Conversely, teams routinely declare victory on a "significant" result that reflects a mix shift rather than a process change.

Program rule: every hypothesis test presented at a tollgate must report (1) the p-value, (2) the effect size with a confidence interval, and (3) a pre-declared practical significance threshold set at charter. A result that is statistically significant but below the practical threshold is reported as a null finding.

Setting the practical significance threshold
Work backwards from the benefit model: Contacts/year 3,200,000 Fully-loaded cost/agent-hr $38 Charter benefit target $400,000/yr Required AHT reduction = ($400,000 / $38) × 3600 / 3,200,000 = 11.8 seconds → Practical significance threshold = 12 seconds. Declare this in the charter. Anything smaller is a null result regardless of its p-value.

Support question → test mapping
Business questionTestNotes
Did the new macro library change handle time?Mann–Whitney (or 2-sample t on log-transformed data) AHT is skewed; use the median. Stratify by contact reason first or you are testing mix, not method.
Is FCR different between two sites?2-proportion test Check the normal approximation (np and n(1−p) ≥ 5); use Fisher's exact for small counts.
Does CSAT differ across four teams?Kruskal–Wallis, or chi-square on top-box CSAT is ordinal. ANOVA on a 1–5 scale is common practice and defensible for large samples but should be flagged; prefer top-box proportion testing.

Three errors to inoculate against explicitly
  1. Testing before stratifying. The most common support analysis error, full stop. Aggregate AHT comparisons across periods are almost always contaminated by contact-mix shift.
  2. Treating agents as independent observations. Contacts within an agent are correlated. With 40 agents × 500 contacts, the effective sample size for an agent-level effect is closer to 40 than 20,000. Use agent-level aggregates or a mixed model.
  3. Ignoring time-series structure. Support data has strong day-of-week and intraday autocorrelation. Two independent-samples tests on consecutive weeks violate independence. Match day-of-week, or use a control chart / interrupted time-series design instead.

Most important support outcomes are binary: did it resolve first contact? did it reopen? did it escalate? did the customer churn? did the SLA breach? Confounding, the support edition

"Longer handle time correlates with higher CSAT — so we should slow down." This is confounded by contact complexity: hard problems take longer and generate more grateful customers when solved. Teach participants to always ask "what third variable drives both?" and to control for it explicitly. The countermeasure hierarchy — teach this ranking explicitly
  1. Eliminate the demand (fix the upstream cause so the contact never happens) — most durable, highest value

Overdispersion — the SPC problem nobody warns support teams about

A standard p-chart assumes the only source of variation is binomial sampling error. At n = 8,000 contacts per day, binomial limits around a 72% FCR are roughly ±1.5 percentage points. Real daily FCR moves ±4 points because of contact mix, staffing mix and day-of-week — none of which is binomial noise. The result is a chart where 60% of points are "out of control," the team learns to ignore it, and SPC is discredited in the organization within a month.

Remedy: use a Laney p′ chart, which inflates the limits using the observed between-subgroup variation (σz). Alternatively, chart at a subgroup level where the extra-binomial variation is genuinely absent, or chart the residuals from a model that already accounts for mix. Teach this in the same session as the p-chart, not as an advanced footnote.

Run rules and the false-alarm budget

Each Nelson rule has its own false-alarm rate; applying all eight simultaneously produces a false signal roughly every 90–100 points even on a perfectly stable process. On a daily chart that is an alarm every four to five months per chart — but a support org running 40 charts will see one almost every day. Choose a deliberate subset (typically rules 1, 2 and 5) and state it in the control plan.

The 6-month re-audit

Build a mandatory re-audit into the program: six months after close, the MBB pulls the control chart and checks whether the gain held. Publish the results. DefineSIPOCA one-page, five-column boundary diagram: Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers.
When to use it

First artifact of any project. Use it to fix scope before anyone argues about it.

How to run it

  3. List 5–7 process steps at a consistent altitude. Not 3 (useless), not 15 (scope too wide).

DefineKano modelClassifies customer needs into must-be, performance and delighter — each of which behaves differently as you improve it.
When to use it

Prioritizing which CTQs to design for, and explaining why improving some things produces no satisfaction gain.

How to run it

  2. For each, ask the functional question ('how do you feel if it IS this way?') and the dysfunctional question ('how do you feel if it is NOT?'), each on the 5-point Kano scale.
  3. Cross-tabulate the answer pair against the standard Kano evaluation table to classify the attribute.

What to look at

Decision rule. Fix all must-be attributes to threshold before spending anything on delighters. A delighter on top of a broken must-be is wasted money.

MeasureValue stream mapA process map annotated with process time, lead time and %complete-and-accurate at each step, yielding Process Cycle Efficiency.
When to use it

Any project where elapsed time, backlog or customer wait is part of the problem.

How to run it
  1. Take a real sample of 20–30 completed units and reconstruct their actual timeline from timestamps.
  2. For each step record: process (touch) time, wait time before the step, and %C&A (share arriving complete and correct enough to work without going back).
  3. Sum touch time and sum lead time. Compute PCE = touch ÷ lead.
  4. Compute rolled throughput yield as the product of all %C&A values.
  5. Rank the waiting states by share of total lead time. This ranking is your project.
  6. Count distinct systems touched and copy-paste operations per unit.
In Python / RFrom timestamp data: df.groupby('ticket_id').apply(reconstruct_timeline), then sum touch vs elapsed. Report the median timeline, not the mean.
What to look at

Support PCE is typically 1–8%. The 92–99% is waiting, and that is where the opportunity is.

Decision rule. Any single waiting state consuming more than ~10% of lead time is a project in its own right.
Support-specific caution. This single calculation reframes almost every support conversation: the opportunity is in the queue, not in the agent's talk time. Lead by presenting it.

MeasureNormality assessmentDetermining whether data is close enough to normal for parametric methods, using probability plots plus a formal test.
When to use it

How to run it

  2. Run Anderson–Darling (better tail sensitivity) or Shapiro–Wilk (better for n < 50).

What to look at

On a probability plot, a right-skewed distribution curves upward away from the line at the top end — that is your SLA-breaching tail.

Decision rule. At n = 50,000, Anderson–Darling returns p < 0.005 for a visually near-normal distribution. Do not let the test override the plot.

MeasureAttribute Agreement AnalysisValidates a human-judged measurement: do appraisers agree with themselves, with each other, and with the truth?
When to use it

Any metric produced by human judgement — QA scoring, disposition coding, intent tagging, severity classification, escalation decisions.

How to run it
  1. Establish the standard first: a senior panel agrees the correct answer for each sample item, before the study, and it is never shown to appraisers.
  2. Build a stratified sample, not a random one: roughly 20 clear pass, 20 clear fail, 10 genuinely ambiguous.
  3. Include every appraiser who scores that queue — minimum 3.
  4. Two replicates per appraiser, separated by at least 5 days, order randomized, blinded to their prior score.
  5. Compute within-appraiser agreement, between-appraiser agreement, agreement with standard, and kappa.
  6. Compute kappa per rubric item, not just overall. This is the actionable output.
  7. Remediate the failing items — rewrite, add decision rules, merge or delete — then re-test.
In MinitabStat → Quality Tools → Attribute Agreement Analysis. Data layout: one row per (appraiser, item, replicate) with the rating and the known standard.
In Python / Rfrom statsmodels.stats.inter_rater import fleiss_kappa, aggregate_raters. For two raters: sklearn.metrics.cohen_kappa_score(a, b). Compute per-item kappa in a loop over rubric columns.
What to look at

Report raw agreement AND kappa together. >0.90 excellent · 0.75–0.90 good · 0.40–0.75 marginal (never use for individual performance management) · <0.40 unusable.

Decision rule. Below 0.40, halt any use of that data in decisions until the measurement system is fixed. Do not proceed to analysis on it.
Support-specific caution. Two traps. The prevalence paradox: if 95% of items pass, two appraisers who say 'pass' to everything show 95% agreement and near-zero kappa — which is why the sample must be stratified. And rubric items are not equally reliable: 'verified customer identity' typically scores >0.9 while 'demonstrated empathy' scores 0.2–0.4. The fix for an unmeasurable item is deleting or rewriting it, not more calibration meetings.

MeasureGage R&R (ANOVA method)Partitions measurement variation into repeatability (same appraiser) and reproducibility (between appraisers) against part-to-part variation.
When to use it

Continuous measurements — most often observer-timed task studies in support, or validating a timestamp-derived metric.

How to run it
  1. Select 10+ items spanning the real range of variation. Narrow-range parts inflate the %study-variation and make a good gage look bad.
  2. Use 3 appraisers, 2–3 replicates, randomized order, appraisers blind to prior readings.
  3. Run the crossed ANOVA method (not the older X̄-R method).
  4. Read %contribution, %study variation and number of distinct categories.

ControlKanban / WIP limitsCaps the amount of work in progress, which by Little's Law directly caps lead time.

ControlLaney p′ and u′ chartsAttribute control charts corrected for overdispersion — extra variation beyond binomial or Poisson sampling error.
When to use it

The default attribute chart for support. Daily FCR%, SLA-met%, QA pass%, reopen%, reopens per 100 contacts.

How to run it
  1. Compute the standard p-chart first, to see the problem.
  2. Standardize each point: zᵢ = (pᵢ − p̄) ÷ √(p̄(1−p̄)/nᵢ).
  3. Compute σ_z = mean moving range of the z values ÷ 1.128.
  4. Inflate the limits: p̄ ± 3·σ_z·√(p̄(1−p̄)/nᵢ).
  5. Report σ_z alongside the chart so readers know how much correction was applied.
In MinitabStat → Control Charts → Attributes Charts → Laney P′ (or Laney U′). Minitab reports σ_z automatically.
In Python / RImplement the five steps above directly — roughly ten lines of pandas.
What to look at

σ_z near 1.0 means no overdispersion and a standard p-chart is fine. σ_z above ~1.2 means the standard limits are too tight and will over-signal.

Decision rule. At n = 8,000 contacts/day, binomial limits around 72% FCR are roughly ±1.5 points, while real daily FCR moves ±4 points from mix and staffing. A standard p-chart will show most points out of control.
Support-specific caution. This is the single most important SPC adaptation for support and it is missing from almost every general Six Sigma curriculum. Without it, the team learns to ignore the chart within a month and SPC is discredited in the organization.

ControlEWMA and CUSUM chartsMemory-based charts that accumulate evidence across points, making them sensitive to small sustained shifts.
When to use it

Detecting slow decay after a project closes, or confirming a step change took hold.

How to run it
  1. EWMA: choose λ (0.1–0.3; smaller = more memory, more sensitive to small shifts, slower to react to large ones). λ = 0.2 is a good default.
  2. Compute zᵢ = λxᵢ + (1−λ)zᵢ₋₁, starting from the target or baseline mean.
  3. Set limits as μ ± 3σ√(λ/(2−λ)·[1−(1−λ)^2i]).
  4. CUSUM: accumulate deviations from target; set the reference value k (usually half the shift you want to detect) and the decision interval h (usually 4–5).
  5. Run alongside the Shewhart chart, not instead of it.
In MinitabStat → Control Charts → Time-Weighted Charts → EWMA / CUSUM.

Decision rule. Pair EWMA with the Shewhart chart in every control plan for a project you want to hold. EWMA catches drift a p-chart will miss for months.

Cross-cuttingQFD / House of QualityTranslates weighted customer needs into design requirements, and exposes the trade-offs between those requirements.
When to use it

Designing a support process that does not yet exist: new channel, new product line, new market, AI tier, new site.

How to run it
  1. List customer needs (WHATs) with importance weights from VOC.
  2. List design requirements (HOWs) — measurable characteristics of the thing you are building.
  3. Score the relationship between each WHAT and each HOW (9/3/1).
  4. Compute weighted importance for each HOW; this ranks your design effort.
  5. Build the roof: score the correlation between pairs of HOWs, positive and negative.
  6. The negative correlations are the trade-offs. Design them deliberately.
What to look at

The roof is the valuable part. Concurrency per agent conflicts with resolution quality; speed conflicts with thoroughness; deflection conflicts with satisfaction on complex issues.

Decision rule. A trade-off you did not design will be discovered in production by customers.
Support-specific caution. Use for a new channel: 'answer fast' → concurrency limit; 'don't make me repeat' → context-passing API; 'solve it, don't deflect' → agent authority matrix and KB coverage; 'available when I need it' → coverage hours and deferred-response SLA.
Cross-cuttingLittle's LawWIP = Throughput × Lead time. Exact for any stable system, regardless of distribution or queue discipline.
When to use it

Any backlog, queue or lead-time question. Constantly applicable and constantly ignored.

How to run it
  1. Measure any two of the three over a stable period and compute the third.
  2. Verify stability first — arrivals ≈ departures over the measurement window, or the law does not apply.
  3. Use it to set a WIP cap: required WIP = throughput × target lead time.
  4. Use it to sanity-check any claim: if someone promises lower lead time with no WIP or throughput change, the arithmetic says no.
In Python / Rlead_time = wip / throughput
What to look at

4,200 open tickets ÷ 1,100 closed/day = 3.8 days average lead time. Halving WIP halves lead time, at constant throughput.

Decision rule. It is an identity, not a model. It cannot be argued with, only misapplied — usually by measuring over an unstable period.
Support-specific caution. Use the calculator on this page. The most common misuse is applying it to a period where the backlog is growing or shrinking rapidly.
Cross-cuttingErlang C / A staffing modelsQueueing models that relate agent count, arrival rate and handle time to service level, wait time and occupancy.
When to use it

Real-time channels (voice, chat). Staffing, service-level target setting, and understanding why small demand changes cause large service failures.

How to run it
  1. Compute offered load in Erlangs: A = arrival rate × average handle time (in consistent units).
  2. Erlang C gives P(wait) and ASA for a given agent count, assuming Poisson arrivals and no abandonment.
  3. Erlang A adds abandonment and is more realistic for most contact centres.
  4. Add shrinkage: required scheduled FTE = required on-phone agents ÷ (1 − shrinkage).
  5. Model interval by interval (15 or 30 minutes). Daily models hide the problem.
  6. Plot service level against agent count to show the non-linearity.
In Python / Rpip install pyworkforce or implement the Erlang C formula directly; it is a short recursion.
What to look at

Near saturation the response is sharply non-linear: at high occupancy a small demand increase produces a disproportionately large increase in wait time.

Decision rule. Never extrapolate staffing linearly. The relationship between agents and service level is a curve, and it is steepest exactly where most contact centres operate.
Support-specific caution. Erlang assumes Poisson arrivals — independent, memoryless. That breaks badly during incidents, when arrivals cluster hard. Do not use Erlang to plan for outage response.

Statistical test selector

Answer the questions to reach the correct test. Every path ends with the support-specific caution that applies.

Before you run any test
  1. Stratify. Confirm the groups you are comparing have the same contact-reason mix, channel split and customer-segment mix. If not, you are testing mix, not process.
  2. Check independence. Contacts within an agent, and days within a week, are correlated. Aggregate to the independent unit or use a model that accounts for clustering.
  3. Declare the practical threshold before you look at the p-value.
  4. Plot the data first. Every time. A histogram and a time-series plot will tell you more than the test will.

Set Year 1 expectations honestly

  
Year 1 barely pays for itself, because Black Belts complete only one project while learning and
  because the first cohort's projects are the least well-selected. A program pitched on a 5:1 first-year
  return will be judged a failure in month 11 and cancelled before the compounding years arrive. Pitch
  it as a three-year investment with a modest Year 1, a strong Year 2 and replication upside in
  Year 3, and hold the line on that framing when someone asks for a bigger number.


  
Benefit accounting policy — agree this with Finance before month 3

  
    
    
  • Hard savings require a documented harvest: headcount reduction, hiring avoidance
    against an approved plan, outsourcer volume renegotiation, or a spend line that actually falls.
    • 
    
  • Soft savings (capacity freed but not harvested, cycle time, satisfaction) are
    reported separately and never in the headline number.
    • 
    
  • Cost avoidance (compliance, risk) is reported in a third category with a
    stated rationale.
    • 
    
  • Realization discount: benefits are booked at the 90-day measured rate, not the
    pilot rate. Typical realization is 60–80% of pilot.
    • 
    
  • Benefit claim period: 12 months from full implementation. No project claims
    benefit in perpetuity.
    • 
    
  • No double counting: when two projects touch the same volume, the second claims
    only the incremental effect. The MBB adjudicates.
    • 
    
  • Re-audit at 6 months: if the gain has decayed, the booked benefit is revised
    down. Publish the revision.
    •

Where support projects come from

  

    
Contact driver Pareto
Top 20 contact reasons by total handle
      minutes and by cost, not volume. The top 5 usually account for 40–55% of demand.
      Each one is a candidate for elimination, not just optimization.

    
Rework analysis
Reopens, transfers, escalations, repeat contacts
      within 7/30 days. Rework is the highest-yield project family in support because it is pure waste with
      no customer benefit whatsoever.

    
Lead-time decomposition
Where does the elapsed time go? Queue
      time, awaiting-customer, awaiting-Tier-2, awaiting-approval. Each waiting state over ~10% of lead time
      is a project.

    
Variance hunting
Site-to-site, pod-to-pod, agent-to-agent
      performance spread on the same work. A 3× spread between the 10th and 90th percentile agent on the
      same contact type is a standard-work project.

    
Shrinkage and capacity
Adherence, unplanned shrinkage, system
      downtime, after-call work overruns, training load. Usually the largest single untapped capacity lever
      in the building.

    
Customer-reported pain
Low-CSAT verbatim clusters, CES drivers,
      complaint themes, churn-exit reasons citing support. Connects the program to revenue.

    
Agent-reported friction
Agent surveys, exit interviews, "what
      wastes your time" sessions. Agents know exactly where the waste is and are rarely asked in a form that
      produces a project.

    
Upstream defect feedback
Contact drivers traceable to product,
      billing logic, onboarding or notification design. Highest-value projects, hardest to sponsor — needs a
      cross-functional Champion.

    
Compliance and risk
Disclosure misses, data-handling errors,
      regulatory response SLAs, security incidents. Benefit is avoidance, but sponsorship is easy.

Metric dictionary

  
Standard definitions for the metrics a support Black Belt will encounter, with the measurement trap
  attached to each. Adopt these verbatim or amend them deliberately — but write them down, because
  metrics without a written definition are the source of most cross-team disagreement in support operations.

  

    
MetricDefinitionFormulaTrap

    
      
AHT
Average Handle Time		
        Mean time an agent spends on a contact, including talk/chat time, hold, and after-call work
        (talk + hold + ACW) / contacts
        Three teams will compute it three ways. Skewed — report median and p90 too. Falls when volume rises (agents rush) and rises when easy contacts are deflected.

      
FCR
First Contact Resolution		
        Share of issues resolved in a single contact with no follow-up within a defined window
        issues resolved in 1 contact / total issues
        Must be measured at the issue level, not the contact level. Window length (24h / 7d / 30d) changes the number by 10+ points. Survey-based FCR and log-based FCR rarely agree.

      
Reopen rate
        Share of resolved tickets returning to an open state within a window
        reopened within N days / resolved
        Admin edits and merges pollute the numerator. Auto-close jobs pollute the denominator's timestamps.

      
Transfer rate
        Share of contacts routed to another agent, team or tier
        contacts with ≥1 transfer / contacts
        Distinguish transfers (routing failure) from planned escalations (design). Conflating them hides the routing problem.

      
Escalation rate
        Share of contacts requiring a higher tier or specialist
        escalated contacts / contacts
        Often gamed by informal "ask a senior" channels that never get recorded. Check Slack/floor behaviour against the log.

      
Service level
        Share of contacts answered within a threshold, in real-time channels
        answered ≤ X sec / offered
        A common convention is 80% within 20 seconds, but the right target is a business choice. Whether abandons count in the denominator changes it materially — define it.

      
ASA
Average Speed of Answer		
        Mean queue wait before an agent connectstotal queue time / answered
        Heavily skewed; the mean hides the tail that actually generates complaints. Use p90.

      
Abandon rate
        Share of contacts that leave the queue before being answered
        abandoned / offered
        Short abandons (<5–10s) are usually misdials, not service failures. Exclude them or report both.

      
Occupancy
        Share of logged-in, available time an agent spends handling contacts
        handle time / (handle + available)
        Sustained occupancy above roughly 85–90% is strongly associated with burnout and attrition. It is a constraint, not a target to maximize.

      
Utilization
        Handle time as a share of total paid timehandle time / paid time
        Frequently confused with occupancy. Utilization includes shrinkage; occupancy does not.

      
Shrinkage
        Paid time not available for handling contacts
        (paid − available) / paid
        Typically 25–35%. Split planned (breaks, training, meetings) from unplanned (absence, outages, overruns) — they have completely different root causes and owners.

      
Adherence
        Share of scheduled time an agent is where the schedule says
        adherent minutes / scheduled minutes
        High adherence with poor service level means the forecast is wrong, not the agents.

      
Forecast accuracy
        Deviation between forecast and actual volume
        1 − |actual − forecast| / actual
        Measure at the interval level, not daily. Daily accuracy can be excellent while every interval is wrong in alternating directions.

      
CSAT
        Satisfaction with a specific interaction, usually top-box or top-two-box on a 5-point scale
        top-box responses / responses
        5–20% response rate, strongly bimodal. Abandoned contacts are never surveyed. Agent-solicited surveys are systematically inflated. Ordinal — use proportion tests, not means.

      
CES
Customer Effort Score		
        Perceived effort required to get the issue resolvedmean or top-box of effort item
        More predictive of repeat contact and disloyalty than CSAT for service interactions. Sensitive to question wording — do not change the wording mid-study.

      
NPS
        Relationship-level loyalty measure%promoters − %detractors
        A relationship metric being used as a transactional one in most support orgs. Very noisy at low n; the CI on a monthly team NPS is usually enormous.

      
Contacts per resolved issue
        Contact-to-issue ratio; the inverse view of FCRcontacts / distinct issues
        The single most honest efficiency metric in support, and the least commonly reported.

      
Contact rate
        Demand normalized to the customer basecontacts / active customers / period
        The only volume metric that survives business growth. Absolute volume tells you nothing about whether the product is getting better.

      
Cost per contact
        Fully-loaded cost of handling one contact
        total support cost / contacts
        Define "total": agent salary only, or plus supervision, tooling, facilities, training and QA? A 2× difference between definitions is normal.

      
Backlog age (p90)
        90th percentile age of open ticketsquantile(age, 0.9)
        Report the percentile, not the mean or the count. A stable count can hide a growing tail of very old tickets — which is exactly what generates escalations.

      
QA score
        Rubric-based assessment of contact qualitypoints earned / points available
        Validate with attribute agreement analysis before using it for anything. Typical monthly sample of 4–8 contacts per agent gives a confidence interval too wide for individual performance management.

      
Containment / deflection rate
        Share of self-service or bot sessions that do not result in a human contact
        sessions with no follow-on contact / sessions
        Containment is not success. A customer who gives up is contained. Pair every containment number with a downstream contact-rate and CSAT check.

      
Agent attrition
        Annualized rate of agents leavingleavers / average headcount, annualized
        Split voluntary from involuntary and by tenure band. Attrition in the first 90 days is a hiring/onboarding problem; after 12 months it is a job-design problem.

Plain-English glossary

  
Every acronym and term used anywhere in this program, explained without jargon — what
  it is, why it matters, and where to get the number. 97 entries.

  

  

  
1.5 sigma shift
The 1.5σ shift


A convention that adds 1.5 to the sigma number you compute from real data.
It exists because processes drift over the long run, so a process that looks like 3.1σ in a year of data behaves like 4.6σ in any short window. The 3.4-DPMO definition of 'Six Sigma' assumes it.


How you get this numberZ from your data is the long-term figure. Add 1.5 to get the short-term sigma level that quality tables quote. Say which one you are using — never mix them in one report.
A3
A3


A one-page structured problem-solving format — background, current state, goal, analysis, countermeasures, plan, follow-up.
It is the right weight for a Green Belt or team-lead problem that does not justify a full DMAIC.


How you get this numberFits on a single sheet by design. The constraint is the point — it forces clarity.
Abandon rate
Abandon rate


The share of customers who gave up before reaching an agent.
It is lost demand that usually comes back later, often angrier.


How you get this numberFrom your phone or chat platform. Exclude very short abandons (under 5–10 seconds) — those are usually misdials, not service failures.
Adherence
Schedule adherence


How closely agents follow the schedule they were given.
High adherence combined with poor service level means the forecast is wrong, not the agents.


How you get this numberFrom workforce management: adherent minutes ÷ scheduled minutes.
AHT
Average Handle Time


How long an agent spends on a contact in total — talking or chatting, plus hold, plus the notes and admin afterwards.
It is the main driver of how many agents you need, so it converts directly to money.


How you get this numberFrom your phone system or ticketing platform: (talk + hold + after-call work) ÷ contacts. Check the definition — different teams include different pieces, and three 'AHT' figures in circulation is normal.
Alpha
Alpha (α) — significance level


How often you are willing to shout 'we found something!' when nothing is actually there.
0.05 means you accept a 5% false-alarm rate. Tighten it to 0.01 when acting on the result is expensive.


How you get this numberYou choose it before you run the test. 0.05 is the default; use 0.01 for high-stakes decisions.
ANOVA
Analysis of Variance


A test for whether three or more groups differ on an average.
Comparing sites, teams, pods or tenure bands.


How you get this numberRun the overall test, then a post-hoc test (Tukey) to find which specific pairs differ. Without the post-hoc, a significant result is not actionable.
ASA
Average Speed of Answer


How long customers wait in the queue before an agent picks up.
It drives abandonment and first impressions.


How you get this numberFrom your phone or chat platform: total queue time ÷ answered contacts. Heavily skewed — report the 90th percentile too, because the mean hides the people who waited eight minutes.
Attrition
Agent attrition


The rate at which agents leave, annualised.
It resets every improvement that depends on people remembering something, and it is the main reason training-based controls decay.


How you get this numberFrom HR systems. Split voluntary from involuntary and by tenure band — leaving in the first 90 days is a hiring problem, after 12 months it is a job-design problem.
AUC
Area Under the Curve


A 0.5-to-1.0 score for how well a yes/no prediction model separates the two outcomes.
0.5 is a coin flip; 0.7 is useful for finding drivers; 0.8+ is strong.


How you get this numberCompute on a holdout sample the model has never seen. In-sample performance always flatters.
Backlog age
Backlog age (p90)


How old the oldest 10% of your open tickets are.
A stable backlog count can hide a growing tail of very old tickets, and that tail is what generates escalations.


How you get this numberFrom your ticketing system: the 90th percentile of (now − created) across open tickets. Report this, not the count or the mean.
Beta
Beta (β) — Type II error rate


How often you miss a real difference that was actually there.
A test with low power will tell you 'no difference' when there was one, and you will wrongly abandon a good idea.


How you get this numberPower = 1 − β. Choose power of 0.80 minimum, 0.90 for anything you will act on expensively, then compute the sample size you need.
Black Belt
Black Belt


A full-time improvement lead trained to run complex, cross-functional projects.
They are the engine of the program. Partial dedication is the most reliable predictor of program failure.


How you get this numberRoughly one per 100 support staff. At full dedication, expect 3–5 completed projects a year at $150k–$250k validated benefit each.
CES
Customer Effort Score


How hard the customer felt they had to work to get their problem solved.
It predicts repeat contact and disloyalty better than satisfaction does for service interactions.


How you get this numberFrom your survey tool, usually a single agree/disagree item. Do not change the wording mid-study — it is very sensitive to phrasing.
Champion
Champion


The senior leader who sponsors the project and owns the budget the saving lands in.
Their attendance at tollgates is the single most reliable predictor of whether a project delivers.


How you get this numberUsually an operations director or VP. They select and charter the project, remove blockers, and renegotiate any team targets the project will disturb.
Chi-square
Chi-square test


A test of whether two categorical things are related — for example, whether the mix of contact reasons differs between two months.
This is the mix-shift detector. Run it before any before-and-after comparison, or your whole conclusion may be an artefact.


How you get this numberBuild a table of counts, run the test, then read the standardised residuals to see which categories drove the result. The overall p-value alone is not actionable.
Confidence interval
Confidence interval (CI)


The range the true answer is probably in, given the data you collected.
A single number pretends to a precision you do not have. '72% ±9 points' is honest; '72%' is not.


How you get this numberYour software reports it. If the interval crosses zero for a difference, you have not shown a difference.
Confounding
Confounding


When something you did not account for is driving both things you are comparing.
Classic support example: longer handle time correlates with higher satisfaction — because hard problems take longer and produce grateful customers when solved.


How you get this numberAlways ask 'what third thing could drive both?'. Control for it by stratifying or by including it in a model.
Contact rate
Contact rate


How many contacts you get per customer per period.
It is the only demand metric that survives business growth. Raw volume tells you nothing about whether the product is improving.


How you get this numberContacts ÷ active customers ÷ period. You need the customer count from your business systems, not from support.
Containment
Containment / deflection rate


The share of self-service or bot sessions that did not end up needing a human.
It is the headline number for automation investment, and on its own it is dangerously misleading.


How you get this numberFrom your bot or help-centre analytics. Containment is not success — a customer who gave up is contained. Always pair it with the downstream contact rate and satisfaction.
COPQ
Cost of Poor Quality


Everything it costs you because the work was not right the first time.
It is how you size the prize before a program starts, and it is usually 15–35% of total support cost.


How you get this numberAdd up rework contacts, transfers, escalations, credits issued to fix service failures, complaint handling, and the QA function itself. Agree the cost-per-contact basis with Finance first.
Cost per contact
Cost per contact


The fully-loaded cost of handling one contact.
It is the conversion rate between operational improvement and money, so every benefit model needs it.


How you get this numberGet it from Finance, and agree what is in it: agent salary only, or plus supervision, tooling, facilities, QA and training? A 2× difference between definitions is normal.
Cp
Cp — potential capability


How well the process could fit inside the customer's limits if it were perfectly centred.
It tells you whether the spread is the problem or the aim is the problem.


How you get this number(Upper limit − lower limit) ÷ (6 × short-term standard deviation). Rarely used in support, because support specs are usually one-sided.
Cpk
Cpk — actual capability


How well the process actually fits inside the customer's limits, accounting for how off-centre it is.
1.33 is the usual 'capable' bar. Below 1.0 you are producing defects at a rate customers notice.


How you get this numberThe smaller of (upper limit − average)÷3σ and (average − lower limit)÷3σ, using short-term σ.
CSAT
Customer Satisfaction score


How happy customers say they were with a specific interaction, usually the share who gave a top rating.
It is the most common voice-of-customer metric, and the most commonly misused.


How you get this numberFrom your survey tool. Only 5–20% of customers respond and they are strongly split between delighted and furious, so treat it as a sample with heavy bias — not a population figure.
CTQ
Critical To Quality characteristic


The specific, measurable thing that has to be true for the customer to be satisfied.
It turns 'customers want faster service' into something you can actually put on a chart.


How you get this numberBuild a CTQ tree: customer's words → what has to be true → the measurable characteristic → the target. It is finished only when two people would measure it identically.
CUSUM
Cumulative Sum chart


A chart that adds up how far off target you have been, so a persistent small offset becomes obvious fast.
It is the fastest way to confirm an improvement actually landed on the day you deployed it.


How you get this numberAccumulate deviations from target; set the reference value k at half the shift you want to catch and the decision interval h at 4–5.
DFSS
Design for Six Sigma


The version you use when the process does not exist yet and you are building it from scratch.
DMAIC improves what exists; DFSS designs what does not. Using the wrong one wastes months.


How you get this numberUse it for a new channel, a new product's support model, a new market or site, or an AI support tier. Common frameworks: DMADV, IDOV, DMEDI.
DMAIC
Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control


The five-phase problem-solving sequence Six Sigma projects follow.
The discipline is that you are not allowed to jump to a solution before you have proved the cause.


How you get this numberUse it when the cause is genuinely unknown and the problem is measurable. If you already know the answer, just do it — that is a project plan, not a DMAIC.
DPMO
Defects Per Million Opportunities


The same thing as DPO, multiplied by a million so it is a whole number instead of a tiny decimal.
It is the standard currency for comparing quality across completely different processes, and it converts directly to a sigma level.


How you get this numberDPO × 1,000,000. Use the DPMO calculator on this page — give it units, opportunities and defects.
DPO
Defects Per Opportunity


The share of all the chances to go wrong that actually went wrong.
It lets you compare a simple process to a complicated one fairly, because a complicated process has more chances to fail.


How you get this numberDefects ÷ (units × opportunities per unit). You must agree the opportunity count once, at program level, and never change it mid-project.
DPU
Defects Per Unit


The average number of things that went wrong on each item you handled.
It is the simplest quality measure. If DPU is 0.25, then on average one in four contacts had something wrong with it.


How you get this numberCount every defect that occurred in the period, then divide by the number of contacts (or tickets) handled. One contact can have more than one defect, so DPU can be bigger than 1.
Effect size
Effect size


How big the difference actually is, in the units your business cares about.
At support volumes almost everything is statistically significant. Effect size is what tells you whether it matters.


How you get this numberReport it as the plain difference — '47 seconds faster', '3.2 percentage points higher' — with a confidence interval, not as a p-value.
Erlang C
Erlang C


A queueing formula that tells you how many agents you need to hit a service level, given your arrival rate and handle time.
The relationship is a steep curve, not a straight line — which is why a small demand increase can double your wait times.


How you get this numberUse a workforce management tool or an Erlang calculator, interval by interval. It assumes arrivals are random and nobody abandons; Erlang A adds abandonment and is more realistic.
Escalation rate
Escalation rate


The share of contacts that had to go to a higher tier or a specialist.
Each escalation costs more and takes longer, and high rates often mean an authority or knowledge problem rather than a difficulty problem.


How you get this numberFrom your ticketing system. Beware informal escalation — asking a senior agent on Slack — which never appears in the log and can be 10–20% of resolutions.
EWMA
Exponentially Weighted Moving Average chart


A control chart with memory — each point carries a little of the history, so slow drift shows up.
Ordinary control charts are bad at spotting small, sustained slides. Post-project decay is exactly that shape.


How you get this numberPick λ around 0.2, compute the weighted running value, and set limits from the formula. Run it alongside your normal chart, not instead of it.
FCR
First Contact Resolution


The share of customer problems fully solved without the customer having to come back.
It is the metric most tightly linked to satisfaction, and reducing repeat contacts is usually the biggest available saving.


How you get this numberCount issues resolved in one contact ÷ total issues, within a fixed window. It must be measured per issue, not per contact, and the window (24h, 7d, 30d) changes the number by 10+ points.
FMEA
Failure Modes and Effects Analysis


A structured list of everything that can go wrong, scored so you can work on the worst things first.
It stops teams fixing whatever is most visible instead of whatever is most damaging.


How you get this numberFor each process step: what can go wrong, how bad (severity), how often (occurrence), how likely you'd catch it (detection). Multiply the three for a risk priority number.
FPY
First Pass Yield


The share of items that got through one step correctly the first time, with no rework.
It is the building block of rolled throughput yield, which is what exposes hidden rework.


How you get this numberUnits through the step with no rework, transfer or reopen ÷ units that entered. Rework includes the informal kind — if an agent asked a colleague and redid the work, that is not first pass.
FTE
Full Time Equivalent


One person working full time for a year — the unit capacity savings get expressed in.
It is how operational improvement gets translated into something Finance recognises.


How you get this numberHours saved ÷ annual paid hours per person (commonly 1,700–1,900 after leave). Confirm the figure your Finance team uses.
Gage R&R
Gage Repeatability and Reproducibility


A study that measures how much of the variation you see is really just measurement error.
If a third of your variation is the ruler rather than the thing being measured, every conclusion is shaky.


How you get this number10+ items, 3 appraisers, 2–3 repeats, randomized and blinded. Read %study variation: under 10% is good, 10–30% marginal, over 30% unusable.
Gemba
Gemba


The actual place the work happens — sitting with an agent and watching, rather than reading a process document.
The documented process and the real process always differ, and the gap is usually where your problem lives.


How you get this numberGo and watch. Time the steps. Ask 'what do you do when that doesn't work?'. Ninety minutes of this beats a week of analysis.
Green Belt
Green Belt


A part-time improvement practitioner who runs smaller, contained projects within one team or queue.
They provide the volume of smaller wins and the feeder pipeline for Black Belt projects.


How you get this numberRoughly one per 25 support staff. Typically 2–4 projects a year at $25k–$75k each.
Hard savings
Hard savings


Money that actually leaves the cost line — fewer people, fewer hires, a smaller invoice.
It is the only category most CFOs will count in a program's headline number.


How you get this numberRequires a named harvest mechanism and a signature. Capacity freed but not harvested is soft savings.
Harvest mechanism
Harvest mechanism


The specific way a saving turns into money that Finance can see.
'We saved 13,000 hours' is not a saving. Whether headcount fell, a hire was avoided, or growth was absorbed is the saving.


How you get this numberName one of: headcount reduction, hiring avoidance against an approved plan, outsourcer volume or rate renegotiation, or absorbed growth. Get WFM and Finance to sign it in the charter.
IRR
Internal Rate of Return


The effective annual return the investment earns.
It lets a program be compared to any other investment on a single number.


How you get this numberThe discount rate at which NPV equals zero. Your spreadsheet has an IRR function; give it the cash flow series.
Kaizen
Kaizen


Focused, short-cycle improvement — often a 3–5 day event with a team pulled together to fix one thing.
It is the right tool when the cause is already known and the fix is within one team's control. Faster than DMAIC.


How you get this numberRun it when you do not need statistical proof of cause. If you do need proof, run DMAIC.
Kappa
Cohen's / Fleiss' kappa


A score from 0 to 1 for how much two or more people agree, after removing the agreement you would get by pure luck.
Raw agreement lies. If 95% of calls pass QA, two analysts who say 'pass' to everything agree 95% of the time and know nothing.


How you get this numberRun an attribute agreement study and compute kappa overall and per rubric item. Above 0.9 excellent, 0.75–0.9 good, 0.4–0.75 marginal, below 0.4 unusable.
Laney p'
Laney p′ chart


A control chart for percentages that has been corrected for the extra wobble you always get at large volumes.
Without the correction, a normal p-chart at 8,000 contacts a day shows almost every point 'out of control' and the team stops looking at it within a month.


How you get this numberStandardise each daily percentage, compute σz from the moving range of those standardised values, and widen the limits by σz. Minitab has it built in under Attributes Charts.
LCL
Lower Control Limit


The bottom line on a control chart.
Same as UCL, in the other direction.


How you get this numberComputed from the data, usually the average minus three standard deviations. Never set by management.
Little's Law
Little's Law


Backlog = how fast you close things × how long each thing takes. Always exactly true.
If you want lead time down and your closing rate is fixed, you must cap your open work. There is no third option.


How you get this numberMeasure any two of backlog, throughput and lead time over a stable period; compute the third. Use the calculator on this page.
Logistic regression
Logistic regression


Regression for yes/no outcomes — did it reopen, did it escalate, did it breach, did the customer leave.
Most outcomes that matter in support are yes/no, and ordinary regression is simply wrong for them.


How you get this numberFit the model, then convert the coefficients into odds ratios. 'Each transfer raises the odds of a reopen by 61%' is directly usable by the Improve phase.
Lognormal
Lognormal distribution


A right-skewed shape: most values bunched low, with a long tail of very high ones.
It is the workhorse distribution of support analytics — handle time, hold time and resolution time nearly always fit it.


How you get this numberTake the natural log of your data and check whether that looks normal. If it does, you can analyse in log space or fit a lognormal directly.
Mann-Whitney
Mann–Whitney U test


The version of the two-group comparison that does not assume a bell curve.
It is the default for raw support duration data, which is nearly always skewed.


How you get this numberRun it, then report the two medians and the estimated shift between them — the test statistic itself means nothing to a business audience.
MBB
Master Black Belt


The program's technical authority — owns the curriculum, chairs tollgates, coaches Black Belts and certifies them.
They are the guardian of statistical honesty. Without one, tollgates become status meetings.


How you get this numberRoughly one per 8–10 Black Belts. Usually a Black Belt with two or more years of experience.
Median
Median


The middle value — half your contacts were faster, half slower.
For skewed support data the median describes the typical experience far better than the average does.


How you get this numberSort and take the middle. Always report it alongside the 90th percentile for any duration metric.
MSA
Measurement System Analysis


Checking that your measurement itself is trustworthy before you trust anything it tells you.
In support the measuring instrument is a human — a QA analyst, an agent picking a code, a customer answering a survey. All three have real error.


How you get this numberFor human judgement, run an Attribute Agreement Analysis and compute kappa. For timings, run a Gage R&R. This is the highest-value day in the whole program.
Normal distribution
Normal distribution


The symmetric bell curve. Most classic statistics assumes your data looks like this.
Almost no raw support data does. Handle time, hold time and resolution time are all right-skewed with long tails.


How you get this numberCheck with a probability plot, not just a test — at large sample sizes the formal test rejects even near-perfect data.
NPS
Net Promoter Score


Percentage of promoters minus percentage of detractors, on a 0–10 'would you recommend' question.
It is a relationship metric being used as a transactional one in most support organisations.


How you get this numberFrom your survey tool. Very noisy at low volumes — the confidence interval on a monthly team NPS is usually enormous.
NPV
Net Present Value


The value of future savings expressed in today's money, minus what you spent to get them.
It is how Finance compares your program against every other use of the same money.


How you get this numberSum each year's net cash flow divided by (1 + discount rate) to the power of the year, then subtract the initial investment. Ask Finance for the discount rate they use.
Occupancy
Occupancy


The share of an agent's available time actually spent handling contacts.
It is a burnout constraint, not a target to maximise. Sustained levels above the mid-80s are strongly associated with attrition.


How you get this numberFrom your workforce management system: handle time ÷ (handle time + available/idle time). Note this excludes breaks and meetings — that is utilisation.
Odds ratio
Odds ratio (OR)


How much more likely the outcome becomes when a factor is present or increases by one.
It is the plain-language output of logistic regression. OR 1.6 means 60% higher odds; OR 0.7 means 30% lower.


How you get this numberExponentiate the logistic regression coefficient. Always report the confidence interval with it, and multiply by how common the factor is to see what actually moves the most volume.
Opportunity
Opportunity (for a defect)


One independent way a single contact can go wrong.
Your sigma level moves a lot depending on how many you count, so an inconsistent count makes projects impossible to compare.


How you get this numberAgree a fixed list at program level. In support, 4–8 is typical: routing, diagnosis, resolution accuracy, communication, timeliness, documentation.
Overdispersion
Overdispersion


When your data wobbles more than the simple statistical model says it should.
It is the reason ordinary percentage control charts fail at support volumes and get abandoned.


How you get this numberCompute σz from the standardised daily values. Near 1.0 is fine; above about 1.2 means you must use a Laney p′ chart instead.
p-value
p-value


The chance you would see a difference this big purely by luck, if there were really no difference at all.
Small p (under 0.05) means the difference is probably real. It says nothing about whether it is big enough to care about.


How you get this numberYour statistics software reports it. Always report the effect size and its confidence interval next to it.
p90
90th percentile (p90)


The value that 90% of your cases came in under.
The customers who suffer are in the tail. p90 is where they live, and the average hides them completely.


How you get this numberAvailable in every reporting tool. Report it next to the median for every duration metric — mean alone is misleading.
Payback period
Payback period


How long until the savings have covered what you spent.
It is the number executives ask for first, and short paybacks get funded.


How you get this numberInvestment ÷ annual net cash flow. Under 18 months is generally an easy sell.
PCE
Process Cycle Efficiency


The share of the total elapsed time that anyone was actually working on the item.
In support it is typically 1–8%. The other 92–99% is waiting, and that is where your opportunity is.


How you get this numberTotal touch time ÷ total lead time. Use the calculator on this page.
Poka-yoke
Poka-yoke (mistake proofing)


Designing things so the mistake is impossible, or at least harder than doing it right.
It is the only kind of control that survives staff turnover. Training and reminders decay in about 6–10 weeks.


How you get this numberRequired fields, workflows that only show the correct next step, routing rules that cannot be overridden, forced verification before close.
Power
Statistical power


The chance your test will actually detect a real difference of the size you care about.
A pilot without enough power that comes back 'no difference' has told you nothing at all.


How you get this numberSet it before collecting data (0.80 or 0.90), then use the sample size calculator on this page to find how many observations you need.
Ppk
Ppk — long-term performance


The same idea as Cpk but using your actual, real-world, long-run variation.
This is the honest number for a baseline. Cpk flatters, because it uses the best-case within-subgroup variation.


How you get this numberSame formula as Cpk but with the overall standard deviation of all your data. Use Ppk for baselines unless you can justify rational subgrouping.
Ppu
Ppu — one-sided capability (upper)


Capability when there is only a maximum that matters and no minimum.
Support specs are nearly always one-sided — there is no such thing as answering too fast — so this is usually the right index.


How you get this number(Upper limit − average) ÷ (3 × overall standard deviation). Transform skewed duration data first or you will badly understate your breach rate.
Practical significance
Practical significance threshold


The smallest change that is actually worth the effort, decided before you look at the data.
It is the antidote to a 2-second improvement that is statistically significant across 3 million contacts and worth nothing.


How you get this numberWork backwards from your benefit target: what improvement would deliver the money the charter promised? Write it in the charter and treat anything smaller as a null result.
QA score
Quality Assurance score


A rubric-based assessment of how well an agent handled a contact.
It is used for coaching and often for performance management — which requires it to actually be reliable, and it usually is not.


How you get this numberFrom your QA platform. Validate it first with an attribute agreement study. A monthly sample of 4–8 contacts per agent gives a confidence interval too wide for individual performance decisions.
Realization factor
Realization factor


The discount you apply to a pilot result to reflect what you will actually get at full scale.
Pilot groups know they are watched and outperform. Typically 20–40% of the gain evaporates at rollout.


How you get this numberUsually 0.6–0.8. Agree it with Finance up front. Programs that report gross rather than realised savings lose credibility within about two quarters.
Regression
Regression


A model that quantifies how much each of several factors moves your outcome.
It turns 'we think tenure matters' into 'each 90 days of tenure reduces handle time by 22 seconds'.


How you get this numberFit with your statistics software. Check for multicollinearity, look at residual plots, and always validate on data the model has not seen.
Reopen rate
Reopen rate


The share of tickets you closed that came back within a set window.
It is pure rework — 100% waste with no customer benefit — which makes it the highest-yield project family in support.


How you get this numberFrom your ticketing system: tickets returning to an open state within N days ÷ tickets resolved. Watch for admin edits and merges polluting the count.
RPN
Risk Priority Number


Severity × Occurrence × Detection — a 1-to-1000 score for how dangerous a failure mode is.
It ranks your risks. But never ignore a severity of 9–10 just because the RPN is low.


How you get this numberScore each of the three on 1–10 using an agreed scale, then multiply. In support, detection is usually 8–10 because most failures are only found when the customer complains again.
RTY
Rolled Throughput Yield


The chance an item gets all the way through every step with nothing going wrong anywhere.
This is the number that explains why every step on the dashboard is green and customers are still unhappy.


How you get this numberMultiply the first pass yield of every step together. Four steps at 0.78, 0.94, 0.88 and 0.96 give 61.9% — so 38% of issues take an unplanned path.
Service level
Service level


The share of contacts answered within a set number of seconds.
The classic real-time channel target, and the one most closely watched by operations.


How you get this numberFrom your phone or chat platform. A common convention is 80% answered within 20 seconds, but the right target is a business choice. Define whether abandoned contacts are in the denominator.
Shrinkage
Shrinkage


The share of paid time that is not available for handling contacts — breaks, meetings, training, coaching, absence, system outages.
It is usually the largest and least-managed capacity lever in the building, typically 25–35%.


How you get this numberFrom workforce management: (paid time − available time) ÷ paid time. Split planned from unplanned — they have completely different causes and owners.
Sigma level
Sigma level (process sigma)


A 1-to-6 score for how good a process is. 6 is world class; most support processes land between 2 and 4.
It is the scale everyone in the quality world speaks, so it travels well to executives and to other functions.


How you get this numberConvert from DPMO. The number normally quoted includes a conventional 1.5σ shift — 6σ = 3.4 DPMO, 4σ ≈ 6,210 DPMO, 3σ ≈ 66,807 DPMO.
SIPOC
Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers


A one-page map that fixes where a process starts and stops.
Almost every stalled project stalled because nobody agreed the boundary.


How you get this numberFill the five columns, keeping the process to 5–7 steps. Define the last step before the first — people argue less about endings.
SLA
Service Level Agreement


The response or resolution time you have committed to.
It is your upper spec limit — the line capability analysis measures you against.


How you get this numberFrom your contract or published policy. Confirm whether the clock can be paused, because any clock that can be paused will be.
SPC
Statistical Process Control


Charting a metric over time with statistically derived limits, so you can tell a real change from normal noise.
It stops the single most expensive habit in operations: reacting to random variation as if it meant something.


How you get this numberPick the right chart for your data type, compute limits from the data (not from targets), and agree a written reaction plan for when it signals.
Standard work
Standard work


The documented, agreed best-known way to do a task, at a specified pace.
It is the baseline you improve from. Without it, every agent is running a different process and variation is unmanageable.


How you get this numberWrite it with the people who do the work, from the gemba. Pair it with a system-enforced control or it will decay.
Stratification
Stratification


Splitting your data into meaningful groups before you compare anything.
It is the single most common failure in support analysis. Comparing two periods without it usually means you are measuring a change in what customers contacted about, not a change in how well you handled it.


How you get this numberSplit by contact reason, channel, customer segment, product, agent tenure band, site, day-part and language. Decide the list before you write the query.
t-test
t-test


A test for whether two groups differ on an average.
The most familiar statistical test — and usually the wrong one for raw support duration data, which is skewed.


How you get this numberUse Welch's version by default. For skewed handle-time data use Mann–Whitney instead, or run the t-test on log-transformed values.
Takt time
Takt time


How often you need to finish one item to keep up with demand.
It tells you whether you are structurally under-staffed for an interval, separately from how efficient anyone is.


How you get this numberAvailable agent-seconds in the interval ÷ contacts arriving in that interval. Only meaningful interval by interval — a daily figure hides the intraday problem.
Tollgate
Tollgate


A formal review at the end of each DMAIC phase where the project either proceeds, pauses or goes back.
It is what stops a project reaching Improve with an invalid measurement system or an unproven cause.


How you get this numberScore against the phase checklist. Use three outcomes only — pass, hold, return. A 'conditional pass' category becomes a universal pass in practice.
Transfer rate
Transfer rate


The share of contacts routed to a different agent or team.
Every transfer loses context and sharply raises the chance of a reopen.


How you get this numberFrom your phone system or ticketing platform. Distinguish transfers (a routing failure) from planned escalations (by design) — conflating them hides the routing problem.
UCL
Upper Control Limit


The top line on a control chart — the highest a point can go and still be normal noise.
Points beyond it mean something genuinely changed and is worth investigating.


How you get this numberComputed from the data itself, usually the average plus three standard deviations. It is not a target — confusing control limits with spec limits is a common and expensive error.
Utilization
Utilisation


Handle time as a share of everything you paid the agent for.
Frequently confused with occupancy. Utilisation includes breaks, training and meetings; occupancy does not.


How you get this numberFrom workforce management: handle time ÷ total paid time.
VIF
Variance Inflation Factor


A check for whether two of your predictors are telling you the same thing.
When they are, the model cannot tell their effects apart and the individual coefficients become unreliable.


How you get this numberComputed by your statistics software. Above 5 is a concern, above 10 is a real problem. In support, resolution time and transfer count are usually collinear.
VOC
Voice of the Customer


What customers actually say they need, gathered systematically rather than assumed.
Most support improvement targets are set internally and never checked against customers.


How you get this numberSurveys, interviews, and — support's unfair advantage — mining millions of existing chat transcripts, ticket bodies and survey verbatims you already have.
VSM
Value Stream Map


A process map with the clock attached: how long each step takes, and how long things sit waiting between steps.
It reveals that the customer's wait is almost entirely queue time, not work time — which changes where you improve.


How you get this numberReconstruct 20–30 real cases from timestamps. Record touch time and wait time per step, then compute Process Cycle Efficiency.
WIP
Work In Process


Everything currently open and unfinished — your ticket backlog and pending queues.
It is the lever in Little's Law you can actually control directly.


How you get this numberCount open items at a point in time. Report the 90th percentile age alongside the count — a stable count can hide a growing tail of very old tickets.
